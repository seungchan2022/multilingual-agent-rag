# Human comprehension of AI-produced work: comprehension/cognitive debt and practices that keep the human oriented

Notes on reading this file:
- Each finding is tagged **[empirical]** (a study measured it), **[practitioner opinion]** (experience or argument, no measurement), or **[vendor/tool doc]** (describes what a tool does, not whether it works).
- Findings marked *(fetched)* were read from the page itself. Findings marked *(search summary)* come only from a search-result summary and were not checked against the full text.
- Research date: 2026-10-13.

## 1. What has been written (2024–2026) about comprehension debt, cognitive debt, and understanding/skill loss with AI assistants, and what did the studies actually measure?

### Takeaway
The problem is named and widely reported by practitioners, but the direct evidence is thin: one small randomized trial on coding (Anthropic, n=52) shows lower immediate comprehension when code is delegated, and that the way the AI is used (asking vs. delegating) separates high from low scorers. No study found measures a developer's understanding of their own multi-day agent-built project, which is this project's exact situation.

### Cited Findings

**The terms**
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Margaret-Anne Storey (9 Feb 2026) defines cognitive debt as debt that "lives in the brains of the developers", in contrast to technical debt in the code. She grounds it in Peter Naur's view that "a program is a theory that lives in the minds of the developer(s)". — [Storey, "Cognitive debt"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- **[practitioner opinion, single anecdote]** *(fetched)* Storey's motivating case: a student team in an entrepreneurship course hit a wall around weeks 7–8 and "could no longer make even simple changes without breaking something unexpected". They first blamed messy code; on inspection "no one on the team could explain *why* certain design decisions had been made". This is one classroom observation, not a study. — [Storey, "Cognitive debt"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Storey's follow-up (18 Feb 2026) restates the definition as "the accumulated gap between a system's evolving structure and a team's shared understanding" of "how and why that system works and can be changed over time". The post offers no quantitative data; it collects practitioner reports of "getting lost in their own projects". — [Storey, "Cognitive debt revisited"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/18/cognitive-debt-revisited/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(search summary)* Simon Willison (15 Feb 2026) called Storey's post the best explanation of the term he had seen, and noted that even if agents produce code that would be easy to understand, the humans involved may have "lost the plot". — [Willison, link post](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/15/cognitive-debt)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(search summary)* A 2026 paper by Storey, "From Technical Debt to Cognitive and Intent Debt", puts three debts side by side: technical debt (in code), cognitive debt (in people), intent debt (missing rationale in externalized knowledge). I did not read the paper itself. — [alphaXiv listing 2603.22106](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Addy Osmani (13 Apr 2026) uses "comprehension debt": the growing gap between how much code exists and how much any person understands. His key contrast: technical debt shows up as friction, while comprehension debt produces false confidence ("The codebase looks clean. The tests are green."). His closing principle: "The comprehension work is the job." — [Osmani on O'Reilly Radar](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)

**Empirical studies and their limits**
- **[empirical — RCT, coding]** *(fetched)* Anthropic, "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills" (Jan 2026; Shen & Tamkin). 52 mostly junior engineers learned an unfamiliar Python async library (Trio) and built two features, with or without an AI assistant. A quiz right after covered debugging, code reading, and concepts. AI group averaged 50%, hand-coding group 67% (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01). The largest gap was on debugging questions. The AI group was about two minutes faster, not statistically significant. — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); paper: [arXiv 2601.20245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245)
- **[empirical — qualitative, not causal]** *(fetched)* Same study, interaction patterns. Low scorers (average below 40%): "AI delegation" (n=4), "progressive AI reliance" (n=4), "iterative AI debugging" (n=4). High scorers (65% or higher): "generation-then-comprehension" (n=2; generate code, then ask follow-up questions to understand it), "hybrid code-explanation" (n=3), "conceptual inquiry" (n=7). The authors state this qualitative analysis does not establish a causal link. Subgroups are 2–7 people each. — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- **[empirical — stated limitations]** *(fetched)* Same study: small sample; comprehension measured minutes after the task; unknown whether the quiz predicts long-term skill; one library; and the setup was a chat assistant, not an agentic tool like Claude Code, "whose effects may be more pronounced" (authors' speculation, not measured). — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- **Source conflict:** participant count is 52 on Anthropic's page; ITPro reports 51. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); contradicted by [ITPro](https://itpro.com/software/development/anthropic-research-ai-coding-skills-formation-impact) *(search summary)*
- **[empirical — preprint, essay writing, not coding]** *(fetched abstract)* MIT Media Lab, "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna et al., arXiv June 2025, revised Dec 2025). 54 participants in three groups (LLM, search engine, no tools) wrote essays over three sessions; only 18 completed the crossover session 4. Measures: EEG connectivity, ability to quote one's own essay, self-reported ownership. Reported: weakest connectivity and lowest ownership in the LLM group; "LLM users also struggled to accurately quote their own work". — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- **[limitations of the MIT study]** *(search summary)* Authors' own limits: small sample, one LLM, essay writing in an educational setting. It is a preprint; the arXiv page does not state peer review. Viral figures about it (percent unable to quote, percent drop in connectivity) could not be verified here and are not reported in these notes. — [The Decoder analysis](https://the-decoder.com/mit-study-shows-cognitive-debt-through-chatgpt-heres-what-it-means-in-real-world-practice/); [MIT Media Lab page](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)
- **[empirical — self-report survey]** *(search summary)* Lee, Sarkar et al. (CMU + Microsoft Research, CHI 2025): survey of 319 knowledge workers with 936 examples of GenAI use. Higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking; higher confidence in one's own ability on the task went with more. The work shifts toward verification, integration, and "task stewardship". Everything is self-reported perception (the title itself says "Self-Reported"); thinking quality was not measured, and the respondents were knowledge workers in general, not developers specifically. — [Paper PDF](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf); [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061)
- **[empirical — RCT on speed, relevant for self-perception]** *(fetched)* METR (July 2025): 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real issues in their own repositories, randomized to AI-allowed or not. With AI they took 19% longer. They had forecast a 24% speedup, and afterwards "still believed AI had sped them up by 20%". METR does not claim this generalizes, and now marks the page "These results are out of date". — [METR, July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- **[empirical — follow-up, inconclusive]** *(fetched)* METR's February 2026 update (57 developers, 800+ tasks). Verbatim: for returning developers "we now estimate a speedup of -18% with a confidence interval between -38% and +9%"; "Among newly-recruited developers the estimated speedup is -4%, with a confidence interval between -15% and +9%." **Direction is not settled by the page:** it defines no sign convention. It also says "Our raw results show some evidence for speedup", and it describes the 2025 result as tasks taking "19% longer" (the same post gives that result's 95% CI as +2% to +39%), which suggests negative means less time with AI, i.e. faster; but that is a reading, not a statement in the source. Both intervals include zero. METR calls the new estimate "likely a bad proxy" because developers declined to work without AI and withheld tasks. The study measures speed, not understanding. — [METR, Feb 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

### Inferences
- The one METR number that bears on this project is not the slowdown (outdated, and about speed). It is the perception gap: the developers believed AI had sped them up by 20% while the measurement showed their tasks took 19% longer. That is evidence that self-report ("I feel I follow what is happening") is an unreliable gauge, which supports checking understanding with a test rather than a feeling.
- The Anthropic pattern "generation-then-comprehension" (let the AI produce, then interrogate it until you understand) is the closest studied analogue to a post-issue understanding check. It scored in the high group, but with n=2 this is a hint, not a result.
- Osmani's unattributed line (delegators below 40%, conceptual inquirers above 65%) matches the Anthropic study's pattern thresholds exactly, so it is most likely the same study restated, not a second source.
- All of these studies concern learning or one-off tasks. The claim "a solo developer loses the thread of a multi-day agent project" rests on practitioner reports (Storey, Willison, Osmani), not on measurement.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures a developer's understanding of decisions (as opposed to code) in an agent-driven project, or how that understanding decays over days.
- No study found that tests whether any mitigation (explain-back, quizzes, walkthroughs) restores understanding in AI-assisted software work.
- Storey's arXiv paper (2603.22106) was not read in full; its contents are known here only from a search summary.
- The peer-review status of "Your Brain on ChatGPT" as of October 2026 was not confirmed.
- Recency: the newest source read is from April 2026, and no search was aimed at May–October 2026. These notes are not a verified snapshot of the state of the topic in October 2026; newer studies or replications may exist.
- The direction (faster or slower) of METR's February 2026 estimates is not stated unambiguously on the page; see the finding above.

## 2. Which practices do practitioners recommend to keep the human oriented, and which are aimed at the AI's memory vs. the human's understanding?

### Takeaway
Almost every widely documented artifact (progress files, memory banks, specs, steering files, handoff notes) is designed so the next AI session can resume; they are human-readable but nothing in them makes the human process anything. Practices aimed at the human's understanding exist (walkthroughs, interactive explanations, Claude Code's Learning style, "one human fully understands each change") but are fewer, newer, and backed by opinion only.

### Cited Findings

**Aimed at the AI's memory (the project already has equivalents)**
- **[vendor/tool doc]** *(search summary)* Cline Memory Bank: six markdown files in `memory-bank/` (`projectbrief.md`, `productContext.md`, `systemPatterns.md`, `techContext.md`, `activeContext.md`, `progress.md`). `activeContext.md` holds current focus, recent changes, next steps and is updated after each session; `progress.md` holds what works, what is left, known issues. The stated purpose is letting Cline resume after its memory resets. — [Cline docs, Memory Bank](https://docs.cline.bot/best-practices/memory-bank)
- **[practitioner opinion, hands-on trial]** *(fetched)* Böckeler (15 Oct 2025) on spec-driven tools. Kiro produces `requirements.md`, `design.md`, `tasks.md` plus steering files `product.md`, `structure.md`, `tech.md`. Spec Kit runs Constitution → Specify → Plan → Tasks and produced about eight files per spec. She distinguishes spec-first, spec-anchored, and spec-as-source. — [Böckeler, martinfowler.com](https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Böckeler's criticism is directly about human review load: "I'd rather review code than all these markdown files"; the files were "repetitive, both with each other, and with the code that already existed" and "very verbose and tedious to review". A small bug became four user stories with 16 acceptance criteria. She also "frequently saw the agent ultimately not follow all the instructions". — [Böckeler, martinfowler.com](https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Osmani doubts detailed specs can replace review, because implicit decisions still get made during implementation. — [Osmani](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Storey: "Specifications and documents are not sufficient if they are not living artifacts" that people actively use. — [Storey, revisited](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/18/cognitive-debt-revisited/)

**Aimed at the human's understanding**
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Storey: require that "at least one human on the team fully understands each AI-generated change before it ships"; document the reasoning, not only the change; hold regular checkpoints (reviews, retrospectives, knowledge-sharing) to rebuild understanding. — [Storey, "Cognitive debt"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Osmani: state the intent of a change before it is written; keep a system-level mental model; keep "tests passed" separate from "I understand"; use the AI for inquiry (questions, tradeoffs) instead of passive delegation. — [Osmani](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(search summary)* Willison's "Agentic Engineering Patterns" guide: "When we lose track of how code written by our agents works we take on cognitive debt." He pays it down by having the agent build interactive explanations (e.g. an animation of an algorithm), and argues it matters most for core logic, less for simple code. — [Willison, Interactive explanations](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/interactive-explanations/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(search summary, via third-party excerpt)* Willison's companion pattern "Linear walkthroughs": ask the agent for a structured walkthrough of code you have forgotten or vibe coded. — [excerpt at conffab](https://conffab.com/elsewhere/linear-walkthroughs-agentic-engineering-patterns/)
- **[vendor/tool doc]** *(search summary)* Claude Code has two built-in output styles for this: "Explanatory" adds educational "Insights" while working; "Learning" is learn-by-doing and leaves `TODO(human)` markers for the user to implement. Both produce longer output and use more tokens. Set through `/config`. — [Claude Code docs, output styles](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles)
- **[empirical, weak]** *(fetched)* The only measured support for any of these: in Anthropic's study the participants who asked for explanations or asked conceptual questions were in the high-scoring group (see section 1; small subgroups, not causal). — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)

### Inferences
- Sorting the brief's list by who does the cognitive work:
  - **The AI writes, the AI reads** (memory): progress/status files, memory bank, steering files, handoff notes. The project's `HANDOFF.md` and `decisions/*.md` are in this group by design.
  - **The AI writes, the human reads** (passive): specs, plan documents, per-session summaries, issue closing comments, AI-written walkthroughs, decision briefings. The project's plain-language issue summary is here.
  - **The human produces** (active): explain-back, teach-back, quiz-me, predict-then-reveal, the human writing the summary, stating intent before the work. The project has nothing in this group that runs on a schedule.
- So the gap is not a missing document. Every existing artifact is in the first two groups; the third group, where the human has to generate something, is empty. Section 4 gives the reason the third group is the one that builds understanding.
- Böckeler's review-burden complaint predicts the project's symptom: 140 dense decision rows are a larger pile of AI-written markdown than a person will re-read. Adding another AI-written summary layer adds to the reading pile.
- Willison's walkthroughs and interactive explanations are still "AI writes, human reads". They are a repayment tool for debt already taken on (useful for the ~140 existing rows), not a prevention tool.
- Local option, not a sourced finding: this environment has an installed skill `compound-engineering:ce-explain` whose description offers a predict-then-reveal check-in for diffs. I did not evaluate it.

### Gaps
- No published source found that documents "quiz me", "explain back", "teach-back", or "predict-then-reveal" as a named, tested practice for agentic coding. One targeted search returned only generic guides. These practices appear in the brief and in tooling (the local skill above) but I cannot cite a practitioner write-up or an evaluation for them.
- No source found for the practice "the human writes the session summary and the AI only checks it".
- Geoffrey Huntley and Kent Beck were suggested sources; I did not reach their posts, so nothing from them is reported.
- GitHub Spec Kit and Kiro are described here only through Böckeler's review, not their own docs.

## 3. What does Anthropic's and others' guidance on long-running agent workflows say about human-facing state?

### Takeaway
Close to nothing. Anthropic's two main engineering posts design every state artifact for the next agent context window and do not discuss how the human keeps up; the human-oriented guidance comes from Anthropic's research side (the skill-formation study) and from Claude Code's output styles.

### Cited Findings
- **[vendor engineering guidance]** *(fetched)* "Effective harnesses for long-running agents" (26 Nov 2025): state is carried by `claude-progress.txt` (a log of what agents did), a JSON feature list with a `passes` flag per feature, git history with descriptive commits, and `init.sh`. The stated goal is "finding a way for agents to quickly understand the state of work when starting with a fresh context window". — [Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)
- **[vendor engineering guidance]** *(fetched)* The same post does not discuss human oversight, human review, or whether a human reads the progress file. Its only human-facing criterion is the "clean state" standard: code "appropriate for merging to a main branch" so that "a developer could easily begin work on a new feature without first having to clean up an unrelated mess". — [Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)
- **[vendor engineering guidance]** *(fetched)* "Effective context engineering for AI agents" (29 Sep 2025): structured note-taking means "the agent regularly writes notes persisted to memory outside of the context window" (e.g. a `NOTES.md`, a to-do list); compaction "preserves architectural decisions, unresolved bugs, and implementation details". The human appears only in passing: "Users get continuity without worrying about context window limitations", and file naming helps "both humans and agents". — [Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)
- **[vendor research guidance]** *(fetched)* Anthropic's skill-formation post is where the human side is addressed: cognitive effort, including "getting painfully stuck", appears important for mastery; "AI assistance should enable humans to work more efficiently *and* develop new skills at the same time"; learning modes may help. — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- **[vendor/tool doc]** *(search summary)* Claude Code's Explanatory and Learning output styles are the product-level human-facing mechanism. — [Claude Code docs, output styles](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles)
- **[secondary report]** *(search summary)* InfoQ (April 2026) reports a later Anthropic harness design that splits work among planner, generator, and evaluator agents. I did not read Anthropic's own post on it, so I cannot say whether it addresses human-facing state. — [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/04/anthropic-three-agent-harness-ai/)

### Inferences
- The project's `HANDOFF.md` plus `decisions/` already match or exceed the vendor pattern (progress log + structured state + git). Following harness guidance further would improve the AI's continuity, not the human's.
- The harness posts treat "the next reader" as an agent with no memory. A human returning after two days is in a similar position but cannot ingest 140 rows at the start of each session, so the agent-style fix (read everything at session start) does not transfer.
- Since vendor guidance is silent, any human-facing step here has to be designed from the learning-science side (section 4), and labelled as the project's own reasoning, not as an adopted standard.

### Gaps
- OpenAI's Codex guidance on long-running work and human-facing state was not found or read.
- Anthropic's 2026 multi-agent harness post was not read in the original.
- No vendor source found that recommends a specific human checkpoint cadence for long agent runs.

## 4. What does learning science imply about reading an AI summary vs. producing one's own explanation?

### Takeaway
The classic findings agree in direction: generating an explanation or retrieving from memory builds and reveals understanding, while re-reading does neither and can feel better in the moment. Applying this to AI-written summaries is an inference; none of these studies involved AI, software decisions, or expert adults reviewing their own project.

### Cited Findings
- **[empirical]** *(search summary of abstract)* Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), *Cognitive Science* 18(3), 439–477. 14 eighth-graders were prompted to explain each line of a text on the circulatory system; 10 controls read the same text twice. The prompted group gained more from pretest to posttest, and the students who produced the most self-explanations reached the correct mental model. Small sample, school-age learners, biology text. — [ASU publication page](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/eliciting-self-explanations-improves-understanding/); [full text PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)
- **[empirical]** *(search summary of abstract)* Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255. Students studied prose passages and then either took free-recall tests without feedback or restudied. On a test 5 minutes later, restudying won. On tests 2 days and 1 week later, prior testing "produced substantially greater retention than studying". Exact percentages were not retrieved. — [paper PDF](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf); [WashU profile page](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/)
- **[empirical]** *(search summary, secondary source)* Rozenblit & Keil (2002), *Cognitive Science* 26(5), 521–562. People rated how well they understood devices, then had to write an explanation, then re-rated; ratings dropped after explaining. The effect was specific to causally complex systems, not general overconfidence. — [Wikipedia, Illusion of explanatory depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth) (secondary; the paper itself was not fetched)
- **[empirical — AI-specific, weak]** *(fetched abstract)* The closest AI-era data point: in the MIT essay study, LLM users "struggled to accurately quote their own work" and reported the lowest ownership. Preprint, essays, n=54. — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- **[empirical — AI-specific]** *(fetched)* In Anthropic's trial, comprehension was lower when the participant did not generate the work, and the high-scoring patterns all involved the participant asking for or constructing explanations. — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)

### Inferences
- **Reading a summary tests nothing.** Rozenblit & Keil's method is itself the diagnostic: people only discovered the gap when asked to produce the explanation. A plain-language AI summary that the human reads and nods at is the "before" rating in that experiment. The project's closing comments are well written, which by this logic makes the feeling of understanding stronger without checking it.
- **Producing beats re-reading for later recall.** Roediger & Karpicke's reversal (restudy wins at 5 minutes, testing wins at 2 days and 1 week) maps onto the user's complaint: reading the issue summary feels sufficient at close, and fails days later when the "why" is needed. The final report at stage 8 is the delayed test.
- **Order matters.** If the human reads the AI summary first and then "explains", they are restating, not retrieving. The human's attempt has to come before the AI's version is shown. This is the logic behind predict-then-reveal.
- **Feedback should follow.** Roediger & Karpicke tested without feedback and still found a benefit, but for decisions the goal is correctness, so comparing the human's attempt against the recorded decision row afterwards is the natural correction step. This pairing is my reasoning, not a cited result.
- **Scope limit on all of the above.** The cited studies used students learning text material. Transfer to an experienced developer recalling their own project's decisions is plausible but untested.

### Gaps
- A search summary mentioned a later Cognitive Science Society paper reporting that prior knowledge strongly affects whether self-explanation helps. I could not identify or read that paper, so it is not cited; if true, it would qualify the Chi et al. result.
- "Fluency illusion" (feeling of knowing produced by easy-to-read text) was named in the brief; I found and read no source on it, so it is not cited. The same applies to the generation effect and to teach-back (a clinical-communication technique).
- Rozenblit & Keil is cited through a secondary source only; the paper's exact numbers were not checked.
- No study found comparing "read an AI-written summary" against "write your own, then compare" for any task.
- One LessWrong post in the search results disputes the illusion-of-explanatory-depth interpretation; I did not read it, so the nature of the objection is unknown.

## 5. Which low-cost rituals (5–15 minutes) can a solo developer run at the end of an issue or stage, and what signals show understanding has slipped?

### Takeaway
No source offers a tested ritual for this; what follows is assembled from the cited findings and is the note-taker's proposal. The consistent design rule is that the human produces something from memory before seeing the AI's version, attached to the close steps that already exist.

### Cited Findings
**Warning signals named in sources**
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Hesitating to make changes for "fear of unintended consequences"; a sense the system is becoming a black box; reliance on knowledge held by one party. — [Storey, "Cognitive debt"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* "velocity can outpace understanding"; loss of confidence when changing things; heavier review burden; debugging friction; stress and fatigue. — [Storey, revisited](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/18/cognitive-debt-revisited/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* Nobody can explain design decisions or how the parts fit; approving work one does not fully understand; activity metrics (commits, PRs, coverage) look healthy while understanding erodes; green tests giving false confidence. — [Osmani](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)
- **[empirical]** *(fetched)* Self-assessment is not a usable signal on its own: METR's developers believed they were 20% faster while measured 19% slower. — [METR, July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- **[empirical, self-report]** *(search summary)* Higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking in the CHI 2025 survey. — [Lee et al. PDF](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)

**Practice elements that have a source**
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* State intent before the work is written; keep "tests passed" separate from "I understand". — [Osmani](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(fetched)* One human fully understands each change before it ships; regular checkpoints to rebuild understanding. — [Storey, "Cognitive debt"](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- **[empirical, weak]** *(fetched)* Ask follow-up and conceptual questions after generation (the "generation-then-comprehension" and "conceptual inquiry" patterns). — [Anthropic research post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- **[practitioner opinion]** *(search summary)* For debt already accumulated: ask the agent for a linear walkthrough or an interactive explanation of the core parts. — [Willison, Interactive explanations](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/interactive-explanations/)
- **[vendor/tool doc]** *(search summary)* Claude Code's Explanatory / Learning output styles as an always-on alternative, at higher token cost. — [Claude Code docs, output styles](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles)

### Inferences
All rituals below are proposals derived from sections 1–4. None has been evaluated. Each is written as an extension of an existing step, not a new document.

- **Ritual A — "three lines first" at issue close (about 5 minutes).** Before `/session-close` drafts the closing comment, the human writes from memory: what was decided, why (including the rejected alternative), what comes next. Then the AI drafts its summary and lists where the two differ. The differences are the output. Basis: retrieval before reveal (Roediger & Karpicke; Rozenblit & Keil's method). Where it lands: the human's three lines can go on top of the existing closing comment, so no new file.
- **Ritual B — decision quiz at issue close (5–10 minutes).** The AI picks 3–5 decision rows from this issue (weighted toward ones the AI drafted) and asks "why this, and what was the alternative?" without showing the row. The human answers; the AI shows the row and marks match / partial / miss. Basis: testing effect; Anthropic's finding that question-driven use tracked with higher scores. Count of misses is a number the project could store, which fits its rule that numbers come from saved raw results.
- **Ritual C — predict-then-reveal at session start (2–3 minutes).** Before `/session-start` prints `HANDOFF.md`, the human says where the project is and what the next task is. Then the handoff is shown. Basis: same as A. This directly tests "where are we now?".
- **Ritual D — stage-end teach-back (10–15 minutes).** At the end of each of the ~8 stages, the human explains the stage aloud or in a paragraph as if to the grader: the goal, the 3 decisions that mattered most, one thing that went against expectation. The AI's role is only to ask "why?" follow-ups and point to rows that contradict the explanation. Basis: Chi et al.; Storey's "regular checkpoints". Side effect: these paragraphs are raw material for the stage-8 report, which moves part of the postponed process summary earlier without creating a separate document system.
- **Ritual E — intent line before the issue starts (1–2 minutes).** The human writes one sentence on what this issue should settle and what they expect the answer to be. At close, compare. Basis: Osmani's "state intent first". Cheap, and it makes surprises visible.
- **Backlog repayment (one-off, 30–60 minutes).** The existing ~140 rows cannot be recovered by rituals that start now. Options from sources: an AI-generated linear walkthrough of the decisions grouped by stage (Willison), followed by Ritual B over a sample. Reading the walkthrough alone would be the passive form.
- **Who decided.** The existing "who decided" column makes one signal countable without new tooling: the share of rows per issue that the AI decided or drafted and the human only approved. A rising share is a leading indicator; I found no source proposing this metric, it is my suggestion.
- **Signals that understanding has slipped, adapted to this project** (derived from the sourced signals above):
  - Cannot state why a decision row exists without opening it.
  - Cannot say the current stage and next task before reading `HANDOFF.md`.
  - Approving the AI's closing comment without being able to add or correct anything in it.
  - Asking the AI "why did we do that?" about a decision the human is recorded as having made.
  - Commits and decision rows keep growing while the human's own written output (intent lines, three-line summaries) is zero.
  - Feeling confident. Given METR and Rozenblit & Keil, confidence without a recall check carries no information.
- **Cost/benefit note for the report writer.** A + C together cost under 10 minutes per issue and change no file structure. B and D cost more but produce the evidence a grader would ask for. E is nearly free. Given the user's stated rule of adding one feature at a time and checking it alone, starting with one ritual (A is the smallest) and seeing whether the mismatches it surfaces are real is consistent with how the project already works.
- **Risk.** Rituals add friction at exactly the point where the user wants to move on; a ritual that is skipped twice is dead. Tying it to the skill (the skill asks before it drafts) is what makes it happen without relying on willpower. This is my judgment, not a cited finding.
